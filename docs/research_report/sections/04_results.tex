\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section first summarises the evaluation protocol and compares the models. It then examines feedback ablations, the earlier prototype treatments and attention injection. Finally, it compares the re-trained baselines, examines individual classes and reports how the feedback settings were selected.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{The protocol as it was run}
\label{sec:res-protocol}

All models were evaluated using pooled five-fold out-of-fold predictions at training seeds 42, 1 and 2, with the fold partition fixed at seed 42. We report macro-F1, accuracy and weighted F1. NF-UNSW-NB15 is scored over ten classes; NF-ToN-IoT is scored over eight, excluding \texttt{dos} and \texttt{ransomware} while retaining their edges for message passing. The semantic prototype model is deterministic for these folds and embeddings. All runs use \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1}; Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the reproduction details.

Macro-F1 gives each evaluated class equal weight, so rare attacks remain visible even when benign traffic dominates. The reported standard deviations measure training variation at one fixed fold partition; they do not measure uncertainty from changing that partition. A difference is called \emph{separated} only when its 95\% two-level bootstrap interval excludes zero. This procedure resamples edges and independently draws a training seed for each model over 2{,}000 iterations. The seed-matched bootstrap is a secondary check, and the two-level result decides when they disagree. We compare model orderings across datasets, not their absolute scores, because the evaluated class sets differ. The graph-construction limitation in Section~\ref{sec:method-graph} applies to every result.



\FloatBarrier
\subsection{The model ladder}
\label{sec:res-ladder}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} report the four-stage comparison. Table~\ref{tab:metrics} also shows the trained head alone, alongside both feedback configurations, on all three metrics; Figure~\ref{fig:metrics} plots the stage-level results. With the trained-head consultant, feedback reaches macro-F1 of $0.8341 \pm 0.0042$ on NF-UNSW-NB15 and $0.5044 \pm 0.0080$ on NF-ToN-IoT, compared with $0.7644 \pm 0.0045$ and $0.4521 \pm 0.0114$ with the prototype consultant. The head alone reaches $0.8374 \pm 0.0048$ and $0.5081 \pm 0.0073$. Feedback is separated above the GNN, semantic and fusion stages on both datasets, but not above the head alone. Fusion is not separated from the GNN on either dataset. Table~\ref{tab:comparisons} and Figure~\ref{fig:comparisons} give the stage comparisons. Several evaluated classes contain only 12--40 edges, so these aggregate results should be read alongside the small-class caution in Section~\ref{sec:res-perclass}.

The trained head is therefore essential to interpreting the feedback result. It has the highest mean macro-F1 on both datasets, and adding feedback has not established an improvement over it. The feedback-minus-head intervals are $[-0.0228, +0.0128]$ on NF-UNSW-NB15 and $[-0.0342, +0.0299]$ on NF-ToN-IoT. Replacing the prototype consultant also changed the selected consultation percentile; the comparison cannot assign the gain to either change alone. The change between feedback configurations is separated on NF-UNSW-NB15, but not on NF-ToN-IoT under the primary bootstrap. Prototype feedback is not separated above either structural stage on either dataset; its only separated stage comparison is against the semantic model on NF-ToN-IoT. These findings justify examining the stronger consultant while testing the injection path separately in Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}. Appendix~\ref{app:ladder-detail} gives the supporting intervals and explains why accuracy can hide weak rare-class performance.

\subsection{Feedback ablations and the mechanism in isolation}
\label{sec:res-ablations}

Table~\ref{tab:ablations} compares real advice, feedback off and random advice at each training seed. Real advice scores above both alternatives at every seed on both datasets. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} and Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} then test injection with output fusion disabled. The oracle, which deliberately receives the true label, improves over control by $+0.0369$ on NF-UNSW-NB15 and $+0.1354$ on NF-ToN-IoT, with both intervals excluding zero. The prototype consultant has no separated improvement. The trained-head consultant has no separated improvement on NF-UNSW-NB15 and a separated loss of $-0.0378$ on NF-ToN-IoT. This diagnostic used consultation percentiles of 31 and 25, respectively, rather than the final configuration's 29 and 16; injection scales remained 2.0 and 20.0.

The first experiment measures access to semantic advice through the complete system, so it cannot identify injection as the source of the gain. The second shows that correct advice can help through the injection path, but neither realistic consultant produced a separated gain at the settings tested. The oracle is a diagnostic with deliberate label leakage, not a usable detector. Because the isolation settings differ from the final configuration, this experiment does not directly measure injection's contribution at the final settings. The separate head-alone comparison nevertheless shows no established advantage from the complete feedback model over its consultant. Here \texttt{head\_only} means the graph-only control, not the trained semantic head. Prediction changes between passes show that the edge channel is active; activity alone does not establish better predictions. Appendix~\ref{app:isolation-detail} records the headroom calculation, cross-fitting check and churn evidence.

\subsection{Five attempts on the embedding-only consultant}
\label{sec:res-prototype}

Five changes were tested with the prototype consultant: supervising the uncertainty selector, calibrating the consultant's temperature, weighting advice by class reliability, changing the advice format, and cross-fitting the advice. None established the intended improvement. The first two were tested together over three seeds, with an additional condition that re-selected the feedback settings; none of these conditions separated feedback from fusion. The remaining treatments were single-seed diagnostics and do not support multi-seed conclusions. Table~\ref{tab:treatments} and Appendix~\ref{app:negative} give the settings and results. A separate seed-42 check on the uncertain edges found trained-head accuracy of 0.795 on NF-UNSW-NB15 and 0.648 on NF-ToN-IoT, compared with 0.647 and 0.284 for the prototype scorer (Appendix~\ref{app:complementarity}).

These results motivated changing the consultant. The prototype assigns classes using average embeddings, whereas the trained head learns class boundaries from the same frozen embeddings and labelled training examples. Its stronger predictions on the consulted edges make it a better-supported choice for supplying semantic advice. The failed treatments explain why we investigated that choice; they do not prove that every possible prototype treatment would fail or that class imbalance caused the prototype's weakness. The resulting feedback configuration has higher mean scores, but its consultation percentile also changed, and it does not have a separated advantage over the trained head alone. We therefore retain both consultants in the results and treat the head-alone comparison as necessary evidence when assessing the feedback design.

\subsection{Attention injection}
\label{sec:res-attention}

Attention injection produced prediction churn of exactly 0.0000 in every tested configuration, including an oracle supplied with the true label at $\pm 4$ nats. Its bootstrap differences from control were $-0.0007$ $[-0.0211, +0.0180]$ on NF-UNSW-NB15 and $+0.0114$ $[-0.0083, +0.0307]$ on NF-ToN-IoT, neither separated from zero (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). Unlike edge injection, this variant did not change the predicted classes between passes.

The graph structure helps explain this negative result. Attention changes node embeddings but cannot give different endpoint-derived representations to parallel edges between the same hosts; 58.7\% of NF-UNSW-NB15 edges share an endpoint pair. On NF-ToN-IoT, 62.8\% of edges terminate at a destination with only one incoming edge. For those destinations, the attention softmax is always 1.0, regardless of the injected bias. These constraints explain why the location of injection matters. They do not establish how much the original GNN benefits from message passing; that requires the separate comparison described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-scope}.

\subsection{Re-trained published baselines}
\label{sec:res-baselines}

Tables~\ref{tab:baselines} and~\ref{tab:vsbaselines} compare E-GraphSAGE and TE-G-SAGE re-trained on our aggregated representation. With its published settings, E-GraphSAGE reaches macro-F1 of 0.1194 on NF-UNSW-NB15 and 0.4141 on NF-ToN-IoT; TE-G-SAGE reaches 0.3841 and 0.1931. The tables also report validation-selected settings and variants using our node features. Trained-head feedback is separated above every faithful re-trained baseline, although it is not separated above its own semantic head. Prototype feedback is not separated from E-GraphSAGE on NF-ToN-IoT. On that dataset, neither the GNN nor the fusion stage is separated from E-GraphSAGE with its published settings. These comparisons use our protocol and do not compare against scores reported in the original papers.

Much of the baseline gap depends on representation and training choices. E-GraphSAGE classifies an edge from its endpoints alone, so it cannot distinguish parallel edges with different attributes. This affects 58.7\% of NF-UNSW-NB15 edges and 7.9\% of NF-ToN-IoT edges (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}). Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} and Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} separate the NF-UNSW-NB15 point-estimate increments associated with validation selection, node features and the remaining model change; those increments have no confidence intervals. The NF-ToN-IoT baselines and fusion stage also allowed predictions of excluded classes in the reported runs. An optimistic correction bound remains below feedback except for one node-feature ablation at one seed, whose comparison was already not separated. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-detail} gives this bound and the detailed comparisons. These qualifications prevent the results from being read as a general architectural advantage over the published detectors.

\subsection{Per-class behaviour}
\label{sec:res-perclass}

Figures~\ref{fig:perclasslevels} and~\ref{fig:perclass}, with Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, show where the aggregate scores come from. Feedback exceeds fusion on eight of ten NF-UNSW-NB15 classes and seven of eight NF-ToN-IoT classes. Against the GNN, it is higher on nine of ten and six of eight, respectively. Its gains include $+0.2090$ for NF-UNSW-NB15 Backdoors and $+0.1594$ for DoS, and $+0.0903$ for NF-ToN-IoT \texttt{backdoor} and $+0.2242$ for \texttt{scanning}. Losses against the GNN include NF-ToN-IoT \texttt{mitm} and \texttt{xss}. Feedback also trails fusion on \texttt{password}, at 0.2134 against 0.2996. The trained head alone remains slightly higher in overall mean macro-F1, without a separated difference from feedback.

The gains and losses occur in different classes, so a higher macro-F1 does not mean every attack is detected better. Several classes contain only 12--40 edges, and changing a small number of predictions can move their F1 substantially. None of the per-class differences has a confidence interval. The fusion stage illustrates the trade-off: on NF-ToN-IoT it raises \texttt{password} from the GNN's 0.0401 to 0.2996, while \texttt{mitm} falls from 0.8838 to 0.7082 and \texttt{xss} from 0.3728 to 0.0844. These observations identify where models differ; they do not establish why fusion loses overall or show that imbalance alone causes those losses. Appendix~\ref{app:perclass} retains the class counts and detailed scores.

\subsection{Knob selection}
\label{sec:res-knobs}

Validation-only selection chose consultation percentile $k = 29$ and injection scale 2.0 on NF-UNSW-NB15, and $k = 16$ and scale 20.0 on NF-ToN-IoT. The confidence gate retains half of the flagged edges, giving effective consultation rates of 14.5\% and 8.0\%. At these settings, 190 of 656 edges are flagged and 95 gated on NF-UNSW-NB15; 341 of 2{,}127 are flagged and 170 gated on NF-ToN-IoT. Across the 21 percentile candidates, mean validation macro-F1 ranges from 0.8238 to 0.8277 and from 0.5170 to 0.5232. The six scale candidates span 0.8252--0.8277 and 0.5196--0.5232. Appendix~\ref{app:knobs} reports the sweep summaries.

Both curves are flat relative to the observed training-seed variation, so the selected maximum should not be described as a well-established optimum. NF-ToN-IoT's scale is at the upper boundary of the tested range, and these runs cannot determine whether a better value lies beyond it. We therefore call the settings \emph{selected}, not tuned. Settings were selected separately for each dataset and consultant. The prototype-feedback comparison used percentiles 31 and 25 at the same injection scales, which is why the change between feedback configurations cannot be attributed solely to replacing the consultant.
