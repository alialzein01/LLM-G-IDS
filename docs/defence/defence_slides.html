Good morning, and thank you for being here. My name is Ali Alzein. This work was carried out at CESI LINEACT, for the Research Master in Data Science at the Lebanese University.

The title says LLM-enhanced. LLM means large language model. In plain words, the question of this thesis is: can a language model help a graph model detect attacks in network traffic?

I will explain the idea in four steps, show what we found, and then show one result that did not go the way we hoped. That last result is, I think, the most useful part of the work.

The problem

  • A single connection is often not enough to identify an attack.
  • The pattern of who talks to whom matters too.
DDoS (distributed denial of service): many sources converge on one destination.

Let me start with the problem. Network traffic is a stream of connections between machines. If you look at one connection on its own, it is often impossible to say whether it is part of an attack.

What gives an attack away is the pattern: who is talking to whom. Here are two examples. A DDoS, a distributed denial of service, looks like many sources all converging on one destination. A scan looks like one source touching many different hosts.

In both cases, each single connection looks ordinary. The shape around it does not. So we want a method that looks at the shape.

Here is the whole idea in one picture. Step one: we turn the traffic into a graph. Step two: we read every edge of that graph in two ways, a structural way and a semantic way. Step three: we combine the two readings into one answer per edge.

Step four is the contribution of this thesis. The graph model asks the semantic side for help on the edges it is least sure about, folds the answer back in, and runs again. This can happen up to three times.

I will now go through the four steps one at a time.

Step 1

Build a graph

  • Nodes are IP addresses.
  • Edges are groups of network flows.
  • Task: give every edge a label: benign, or an attack type.

Speaker notes · ≈ 1 min

Step one, the graph. The nodes are IP addresses, so each node is a machine. An edge is a group of network flows between two addresses.

So instead of classifying single connections one by one, we classify edges, and each edge stands for a whole group of flows.

The task is then simple to state: for every edge, say whether it is benign, or which type of attack it is. Everything that follows is about how we label one edge.

Speaker notes · ≈ 1.5 min

Step two. Each edge is read in two different ways.

The first is the structural view. A graph neural network, which is a neural network that passes information along the edges of a graph, looks at the network structure around the edge. The specific model is GATv2 with two layers.

The second is the semantic view. We write the same edge as a plain sentence: which endpoints, which protocol, what role the port plays, how often the flows happen, how much data they carry, and how long they last. A frozen language encoder trained on cybersecurity text, called CySecBERT, reads that sentence. Frozen means we never change its weights; we only use what it already knows.

One important detail. The sentence never contains the attack name. That is enforced by an assertion in the code, so the semantic view cannot cheat by reading the answer.

If asked how the semantic model turns an embedding into a class. It fits nothing on top of the encoder. Within each fold we take the mean embedding of each class, ten class means, and an edge is assigned to the nearest one. That is why its score is identical across training seeds. It is chosen because it adds no classifier of its own, not because it is cheap: with 656 and 2,127 edges, a nearest-neighbour readout would cost nothing extra, but it would depend on which particular training edges lie closest and on a neighbourhood size we would have to select. The fusion model does not use those class scores at all; it takes the raw embedding. The feedback model consults a small trained head on the same embedding, because it needs a per-class judgement it can inject.

Step 3

Combine the two views

  • A gate produces two weights that add up to 1, chosen separately for every edge.
  • The two views are mixed in that proportion. This is the fusion model.

Step three, combining the two views. For each edge, a gate produces two weights that add up to one: one weight for the structural view, one for the semantic view.

The weights are chosen separately for every edge. So one edge may lean on the structure around it, and another edge may lean on its sentence. The two views are then mixed in that proportion.

This combined model is what I will call the fusion model.

Step four is the contribution of this thesis: the feedback loop.

The graph model does not only predict. It also measures how uncertain it is about each edge. It takes the least-confident edges and sends them to the semantic side. The semantic side sends back a judgement for each of those edges. That judgement is folded back into the edge's representation, and the graph model runs again. This can repeat up to three rounds.

How many edges are sent? The top 29 percent most uncertain on NF-UNSW-NB15, and the top 16 percent on NF-ToN-IoT. A confidence gate then keeps only the most confident half of those. So in practice, 14.5 percent of the edges are consulted on the first dataset, and 8 percent on the second.

The model that comes out of this loop is the feedback model.

We used two datasets, to see whether the behaviour depends on the data or on the method.

NF-UNSW-NB15 gives a small graph: 49 nodes and 656 edges, with 10 scored classes. NF-ToN-IoT is larger: 1,501 nodes and 2,127 edges, with 8 scored classes.

The big difference is imbalance, meaning how uneven the class sizes are. It is 12.4 to 1 on the first dataset and 582 to 1 on the second, roughly a factor of 47 apart.

Because of this, the absolute scores are not comparable between the two datasets. What we can compare is the shape of the results: which model sits above which.

Speaker notes · ≈ 1.5 min

Here are the main results. The metric is macro-F1, measured out of fold and averaged over three training seeds. Let me unpack that. F1 is a score per class that balances the attacks we catch against the false alarms we raise. Macro means we average that score over the classes, giving each class the same weight: a 12-edge attack class counts as much as the 1,746-edge benign class. Out of fold means every edge is scored by a model that never saw it during training. A seed is the random starting point of training; we ran three and took the mean.

Now the numbers. On NF-UNSW-NB15: semantic 0.7353, GNN 0.7437, fusion 0.7793, feedback 0.8341. On NF-ToN-IoT: semantic 0.2785, GNN 0.4336, fusion 0.4102, feedback 0.5044.

The shape is the same on both datasets: the feedback model is the best. Notice also that on the second dataset, fusion is below the GNN. I will come back to that.

Why not report accuracy? Accuracy for the GNN is 0.8105 and 0.8739, and for the feedback model 0.8852 and 0.9186. But NF-ToN-IoT is 82 percent benign traffic, so accuracy is misleadingly high there for every model. Macro-F1 does not let a model hide behind the benign class.

Speaker notes · ≈ 1 min

Which of these differences are real? Our rule: a difference counts only if a bootstrap interval excludes zero. Bootstrap means we resample the data many times and look at how much the difference moves. If that range never touches zero, the difference is solid.

Under that rule, the feedback model beats the GNN model, the fusion model and the semantic model, and every faithfully re-trained published baseline, on both datasets.

And one difference does not hold: the fusion model does not beat the GNN model, on either dataset. Combining the two views by itself is not enough.

A note on the baselines. E-GraphSAGE and TE-G-SAGE were re-trained on the same graph, the same folds and the same metric. These are not comparisons against their published numbers, which came from much larger per-flow graphs.

The honest finding

The advice is the bottleneck, not the mechanism

  • Two advisors were tried. With the weaker one, none of those wins hold.
  • Swapping the advisor moved the result by +0.0697 and +0.0523: the largest single effect in the project.*
  • The channel tested alone (output combination off), with a perfect advisor that always knows the true label: a real gain, +0.0345 and +0.1090.
  • With any advisor that could actually be built: nothing distinguishable from zero.
The bottleneck is the quality of the advice, not the mechanism.
* An uncertainty threshold was re-selected at the same time, so the whole gain is not attributed to the advisor alone. A second design, injecting the advice into the attention weights, changed exactly 0 predictions in every configuration tested, even when handed the true label.

Now the finding I consider the most important, and it is a negative one.

We tried two advisors, meaning two versions of the semantic side that answers the loop. The weaker one is the nearest-class-mean scorer from slide 5, the same thing that stands alone as the semantic model. The stronger one is a small classification head trained on the same embeddings. With the weaker advisor, none of the wins I just showed hold. Swapping the advisor moved the result by 0.0697 on the first dataset and 0.0523 on the second. That is the largest single effect in the whole project, larger than any change to the mechanism itself. One caution: an uncertainty threshold was re-selected at the same time, so the report does not attribute the whole gain to the advisor alone.

So we tested the feedback channel on its own, with the output combination switched off, so that only the loop could act. First we gave it a perfect advisor, one that always knows the true label. Then the channel produces a real gain: 0.0345 and 0.1090. Then we gave it any advisor that could actually be built. The gain was nothing we could distinguish from zero.

The conclusion is simple. The bottleneck is the quality of the advice, not the mechanism. The channel can carry good advice. We do not yet have an advisor that gives it.

One more design was tried: injecting the advice into the attention weights, which are the numbers the graph model uses to decide how much each neighbour matters. It changed exactly zero predictions, in every configuration we tested, even when it was handed the true label.

Limitations

Limitations, stated plainly

  • The label helps define an edge. Edges are grouped using the attack label. Every number is a controlled comparison between methods, not an estimate of real-world detection performance.
  • The graphs are small: 656 and 2,127 edges, with several attack classes holding between 12 and 40 edges.
  • Error bars cover training-seed variation only.

Three limitations, stated plainly.

First, edges are grouped using the attack label, so the label helps define what an edge is. Every number I showed is therefore a controlled comparison between methods, not an estimate of real-world detection performance.

Second, the graphs are small: 656 and 2,127 edges, and several attack classes hold between 12 and 40 edges.

Conclusion

What this thesis showed

  • Every edge is read two ways, structural and semantic, and a loop sends the uncertain edges for a second opinion.
  • The feedback model is the best on both datasets: 0.8341 and 0.5044 macro-F1, separated from every other model and baseline.
  • Fusion alone does not beat the GNN, and the loop's gain appears only with the stronger advisor.
  • A perfect advisor gives a real gain; a buildable one gives none. The mechanism is not the limit. The advice is.

To conclude. We read every edge two ways, structural and semantic, and added a loop that sends the uncertain edges for a second opinion.

The feedback model is the best model on both datasets, 0.8341 and 0.5044 macro-F1, and that lead is statistically separated from every other model and baseline.

Fusion on its own does not beat the GNN, and the loop's gain appears only with the stronger advisor. With a perfect advisor the channel gives a real gain; with any advisor we could actually build, it gives none.

So the mechanism is not the limit. The advice is. If this work continues, that is where the effort should go.
